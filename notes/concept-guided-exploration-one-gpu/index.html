Reproducing concept-guided exploration on one GPU

Published on 17 November 2026.

I trained a 1.5B model for 100 steps to write better hints for a 7B model solving hard math problems. Its average reward went from 0.588 to 0.590. The one change I could see was that it learned to always list four hints, cleanly numbered.

The training code was fine. The problem was the reward.

The paper

Beyond Repeated Sampling (Labiad, Kowalski, Schoenauer, Munos, Kempe) observes that asking a model the same hard problem 128 times mostly yields near-duplicates. Their fix: a small concept generator lists a few approaches, a larger frozen answer generator spreads its attempts across them, and the small model is trained with RL on how often the large one succeeds. On their hardest problems, pass@128 goes from 19.0% with repeated sampling to 28.9% with untrained concepts and 39.2% with trained ones.

The problems are 200 DeepMath questions that the 7B model solved at most 2 times out of 8 in a separate screening pass. I excluded yes/no answers: in a sample of 200 problems from the hardest difficulty band, 156 were yes/no, which a coin flip solves. This set is still much less hard for my 7B model than the paper's is for its 32B model.

Training did not move the reward

For each problem, the 1.5B generator writes 6 concept lists, each scored by 16 answers from the 7B model, and a policy-gradient update pushes up the lists that scored above their group's average.

The trained generator does 6 points better than the untrained one at 64 attempts, but the confidence interval starts at zero, and more concepts help at a fixed budget anyway. With one run, I cannot tell better concepts from more of them.

Why: a noisy reward, and a biased one

Noisy. Training only uses the differences between the 6 lists of a problem. I scored the same 120 lists twice with fresh answers. The raw scores agree well (correlation 0.78), but that mostly reflects that some problems are harder than others. The differences agree at only 0.15 and 0.30 in two repetitions: at 16 answers per list, the training signal is mostly noise.

Biased. The paper scores a list by the highest success rate among its concepts (max-of-mean), and notes that a maximum over noisy estimates is biased upward. With 16 answers that bias is large. If every concept has the same true success rate:

Listing more concepts pays even when they are no better. My generator did move toward four concepts, which fits this, but its reward did not go up, so this is not proof that it exploited the bias.

The paper uses 128 answers per list, where both problems shrink. What interests me is the low-compute case: can a reward built from 16 or 32 answers be reliable and fair to short concept lists? Next, I will compare reward estimators and bandit-style allocation of answers on fixed pools of answers.

Limits

  • Small models, an easier problem set, and different sampling settings than the paper. This is not a replication of its numbers.
  • One training run, one seed; answers graded by a rule-based checker (math-verify).
  • The two agreement measurements (0.15 and 0.30) are imprecise: only 20 problems.
