Why “better concepts” and “more concepts” cannot be told apart at a fixed budget

Published on 22 November 2026.

Part 5 of 8 in Questions on rewards built from a few rollouts.

After training, the 1.5B concept generator does better than before training on 200 held-out problems:

Attempts kBefore training, pass@kAfter training, pass@k
430.0%32.3%
6468.5%74.5%

The difference at 64 attempts is 6.0 points, with a paired bootstrap 95% interval of [0.0, 12.0]. Even taken at face value, it has two possible causes.

Two explanations for the same number

Training changed the lists in one visible way: the average number of concepts went from 3.3 to 4.0.

  • Better concepts. The generator learned which approaches make the answer model succeed.
  • More concepts. At a fixed number of attempts, the answers are spread across the concepts in turn. A longer list spreads them wider, which helps even if no concept is better than before. The paper reports this effect itself: more concepts improve pass@k at a fixed budget.

A pass@k comparison at equal budget measures the sum of the two. And Part 4 gives a reason to expect the second one: the training reward pays for longer lists.

What would separate them

  • Compare lists of the same length: force both generators to write exactly 4 concepts, or cut the trained generator's lists to the lengths of the untrained one's.
  • Compare per concept: the success rate of the answer model when it follows one concept, before and after training, with the same number of answers for each.
  • Repeat the training. This is one run with one seed.

I have done none of these yet. One more comparison points the same way: the trained 1.5B generator is not distinguishable, at any number of attempts, from the untrained 7B model writing its own concepts.

Takeaway

When training changes how many concepts a generator writes, a gain in pass@k at a fixed budget is not evidence that the concepts got better. The number of concepts has to be held fixed before the comparison means that.
