A signal-to-noise explanation

Two facts stand out in the table. First, the directional filter is consistently the primary driver: on its own it accounts for two-thirds to three-quarters of the solo gains, whereas adding the GNN directly onto vanilla LoRA moves BoolQ by only \(+0.3\) points and CIFAR-100 by \(+0.4\). Second, adding the GNN after filtering yields an extra \(+1.2\), \(+1.5\), and \(+3.0\)—roughly triple to four times what the GNN achieved on unfiltered updates.

The linearised view from Part 7 clarifies this asymmetry. Suppose each unfiltered layer update decomposes into task signal plus nuisance noise, \(\Delta W_\ell = S_\ell + N_\ell\). Applying a smoothing operator across layers without filtering first mixes both components:

\[(I - \eta L)(S + N) = (I - \eta L)S + (I - \eta L)N.\]

When \(N_\ell\) is large—or worse, when semantic similarity edges are constructed from noisy batch gradients—message passing spreads task-irrelevant variance across neighbouring layers rather than reinforcing coherent task directions. Once Stage-1 gating suppresses \(N_\ell\) inside each layer, the node features entering the GNN have a substantially higher signal-to-noise ratio, and cross-layer alignment propagates cleaner task updates across depth.

Takeaway

Within-layer filtering and cross-layer coordination are asymmetric rather than interchangeable: denoising each layer first is a precondition for cross-layer smoothing to pay off.
